# Why You Can't Tickle Yourself (Bean Stories short #8)

## Facts (sourced)
- Self-produced touch is rated less ticklish than the identical touch from a robot; the cerebellum predicts the
  sensory consequences of your own movement and that prediction cancels/attenuates the response. Blakemore, Wolpert &
  Frith (UCL, London), "Central cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation", Nature Neuroscience 1998; Blakemore et
  al. 2000 (cerebellum fMRI); Scientific American "Why can't a person tickle himself?".
- Robot set-up: participants moved a robot arm with one hand; a second robot moved a soft foam piece across the other
  palm. Computer-controlled delays of 0/100/200/300 ms between the movement and the touch: the longer the delay, the
  more tickly it felt. Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert 1999, J. Cognitive Neuroscience ("Spatio-temporal prediction
  modulates the perception of self-produced stimuli").

## Voice-over (Liam)
Try to tickle yourself. Go on, I'll wait.
Nothing, right? That's not you. That's your brain.
Every time you move, a part of your brain called the cerebellum predicts exactly what you're about to feel.
And then it turns that feeling down.
So when your own fingers come for your ribs, your brain already knows. No surprise. No tickle.
But scientists in London wanted to beat the brain.
So they built a tickle robot.
You move a handle with one hand. The robot tickles your other hand.
At first, it barely tickled.
Then they added a tiny delay. Just a fraction of a second.
And suddenly... it tickled.
The longer the delay, the more it tickled.
Your brain just couldn't keep up.
So if you ever want to tickle yourself... you'll need a robot. A slightly slow one.
