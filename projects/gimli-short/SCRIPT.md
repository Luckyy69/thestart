# "The Jet That Ran Out of Fuel" (the Gimli Glider) — original script v1 (2026-11-23)
Facts (sources):
- Air Canada Flight 143, Boeing 767, 23 July 1983, Montreal -> Ottawa -> Edmonton; 61 passengers + 8 crew = 69 people. (Wikipedia "Gimli Glider"; CBC Archives)
- Canada was converting to metric; the new 767 was the airline's first aircraft calibrated in metric units (kg). (Wikipedia; CBC Archives)
- Fuel-quantity gauges were inoperative, so fuel was dipped and converted by hand; the crew used the pounds-per-litre
  density (1.77) instead of kg-per-litre (0.8), so the plane carried ~22,300 lb instead of the 22,300 kg needed - less than half. (Wikipedia; Athabasca Univ.)
- Both engines flamed out at ~41,000 ft; most electronic cockpit displays went blank. (Wikipedia; AOPA Pilot 2000)
- Captain Bob Pearson was an experienced glider pilot; F/O Maurice Quintal had served at RCAF Station Gimli. (Wikipedia; CBC)
- Gimli's old runway had been turned into a motor-racing site; that evening the Winnipeg Sports Car Club's Family Day filled it
  with families, campers and go-kart races. (Wikipedia "RCAF Station Gimli"; Global News)
- Pearson used a forward slip (a glider technique, never before done in a 767) to lose height without gaining speed. (Wikipedia; AOPA)
- Nose gear did not lock and collapsed; the nose scraped the runway; club members put out the smoke with fire extinguishers. (Wikipedia; Global News)
- All 69 survived, only minor injuries in the evacuation. The aircraft was repaired and flew until 2008. (Wikipedia; Airline Geeks)

## Voice-over (English, calm steady tone)
A passenger jet ran out of fuel at forty-one thousand feet, because someone used the wrong units.
It's 1983, and Canada is switching to the metric system.
Air Canada's brand new Boeing 767 measures its fuel in kilograms.
But on the ground, the fuel is worked out in pounds.
It's like a recipe that says two cups of flour, and you add two spoons. The number looks right, but there's far too little.
So the plane takes off with less than half the fuel it needs.
High over Canada, a warning beeps. Then the left engine stops. Then the right one.
Most of the screens in the cockpit go dark.
Luckily, the captain flies gliders as a hobby.
Unluckily, the only runway they can reach has become a racetrack.
And tonight, it's full of families, campers and go-karts.
So the captain tips the jet sideways, a glider trick nobody had ever tried in an airliner, and it drops like a stone without speeding up.
The front wheel collapses, and the nose scrapes down the runway in a shower of sparks.
The racers run over with fire extinguishers.
All sixty-nine people on board walk away.
And the plane? It was repaired, and flew for another twenty-five years, as the Gimli Glider.
