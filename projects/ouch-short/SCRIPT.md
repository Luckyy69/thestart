# Why We Say "Ow" (Bean Stories short #7)

## Facts (sourced)
- Saying "ow" raised pain tolerance in the cold-pressor (ice water) test; hearing a recording of "ow" did not help; pressing
  a button helped similarly -> the act itself, not the sound. Swee & Schirmer (National University of Singapore),
  Journal of Pain 2015; IASP "Exclaiming and pain tolerance"; Discover Magazine. Effect estimated ~20%.
- Pain interjections in 131 languages contain far more [a]-like vowels/diphthongs ("ay", "ow", "aie") than expected.
  Ponsonnet et al., JASA 2024 (UWA news, Scientific American, ScienceDaily).
- Swearing raised pain tolerance ~33% vs a neutral word; made-up swear words ("fouch", "twizpipe") did not work.
  Stephens & Robertson, Frontiers in Psychology 2020 (Keele University). Stephens et al. 2009 NeuroReport.
- People who swear more often in daily life got less benefit. Stephens & Umland 2011.

## Voice-over (Liam)
Ever hit your toe and yelled "OW!" before you even knew what happened?
Your brain is fast. But it might also be a little dramatic.
A pain signal races from your toe to your brain in a split second, and your mouth reacts before you can even think.
And here's the weird part: that "ow" actually makes it hurt less.
In English, it's "ow." In French, "aïe." In Spanish, "ay." In Danish, "av."
Scientists checked a hundred and thirty-one languages. A surprising number share the same "ah" sound.
Then a team in Singapore tried something mean.
They made people hold their hand in ice water, as long as they could.
The people who said "ow" lasted about twenty percent longer.
Hearing someone else say "ow"? Didn't help. You have to say it yourself.
But another team found something even stronger.
Swearing.
People who swore held on about a third longer.
Made-up swear words? Didn't work.
And people who swear all day? They get less of a boost.
So save your swear words... for your toes.
