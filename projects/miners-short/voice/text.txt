Thirty-three men were trapped seven hundred meters underground. And for seventeen days, the world thought they were dead. It's August twenty ten, in Chile's Atacama Desert. Deep inside a mine, the mountain collapses. The only way out is sealed by a block of rock twice as heavy as the Empire State Building. In a tiny shelter, the men find emergency food meant to last two days. So they share it: two spoonfuls of tuna each, every other day. Up on the surface, families pitch tents and wait. Drills punch thin holes into the mountain, searching blind. Day after day, nothing. Then, on day seventeen, a drill comes back up with a note taped to it. In red ink: "We are fine in the shelter. The thirty-three." So why did it take two more months to get them out? Because the rescue hole could only be about as wide as a car tire. So engineers built a skinny steel capsule called the Phoenix, just wide enough for one man. One by one, all thirty-three rode it up into the light. After sixty-nine days, every single one of them came home.
